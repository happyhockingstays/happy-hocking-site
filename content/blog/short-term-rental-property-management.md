Short-term rental property management is the work of running a vacation home for its owner: answering guests, keeping the calendar and prices right, cleaning between stays and fixing what breaks. Choosing a manager comes down to 3 things: who does that work, who holds your listing and your money and how you'll know it's going well.

We manage vacation homes in the Hocking Hills as co-hosts. Here's what we'd want any owner to know before they sign with anyone, us included.

## What short-term rental property management covers

A full-service manager handles nearly everything between the booking and the next booking:

- **Guests:** questions before booking, check-in details, messages during the stay and reviews after
- **Calendar and pricing:** rates that move with weekends, seasons and holidays, and a calendar that stays current everywhere the home is listed
- **Cleaning and turnover:** a full clean after every stay, fresh linens and a restock. See our [vacation rental cleaning](/blog/vacation-rental-cleaning) checklist for what that should include
- **Maintenance:** the hot tub, the pool, the HVAC and the repairs that can't wait
- **The listing:** photos, descriptions, house rules and the booking sites themselves
- **Taxes and rules:** local registration and lodging tax filings, which vary by county
- **Reporting:** a clear monthly picture of bookings, payouts and costs
- **One point of contact:** a person you can call, who knows your home and answers when something needs a decision

If you've been searching for an Airbnb property manager, it's the same job. Many managers list a home on several booking sites, and some run a direct-booking site too.

## Full-service manager or co-host?

There are 2 common setups, and the difference matters more than most owners expect.

- **A traditional manager** often holds the listing under their own account, collects the booking money and sends you what's left with a statement.
- **A co-host** works on your listing. It stays in your name, booking payments go straight to you and the co-host is paid for their work.

Both can run a home well. Co-hosting keeps more control in your hands, especially your reviews and your booking history if you ever switch. Our guide to [vacation rental co-hosting](/blog/vacation-rental-co-hosting) walks through it side by side.

## How to find the best vacation rental management companies for your home

Lists of the best vacation rental management companies are a starting point, not an answer. The best fit is the one that knows your area and can run your kind of home. A big group cabin with a pool and a hot tub needs a different team than a studio in town.

Look for:

1. **Local people.** Who's on the ground, and how far are they from your home?
2. **Homes like yours.** Ask which homes they run now, and look at those listings and their reviews.
3. **Their own cleaning team, or a clear plan.** Cleaning is where guest reviews are won and lost.
4. **Clear pricing.** How the fee is figured, what it covers and what's billed on top.
5. **A written agreement.** Read the term, the notice period and what happens to future bookings if you part ways.

## Questions to ask a vacation rental manager

Bring these to every intro call:

- Who will my guests talk to, and how fast do you reply?
- Who cleans, and how do you check the work?
- How do you handle the hot tub, the pool and repairs between stays?
- Whose name is on the listing, and who receives the booking payouts?
- How do you set prices, and can I see the reasoning?
- What do I get each month, and how often will we talk?
- Who registers the home and files the lodging tax?
- Can I talk with an owner you work for now?

A vacation rental property manager who answers these plainly, without hedging, is a good sign.

## Red flags

- They won't put the fee and what it includes in writing
- The listing has to move to their account, with no plan for giving it back
- They can't tell you who cleans
- Payouts arrive late or you can't see what came in
- Guest reviews mention the same problem again and again

## Switching managers

Owners switch for the usual reasons: slow replies, uneven cleaning, surprise charges or a home that isn't booking like it should. If that's you:

1. Read your agreement for the notice period and how future bookings are handled.
2. Line up the new manager first, so the home is never without someone local.
3. Make sure you have access to the listing, the booking sites and any smart locks.
4. Ask the old manager for the home's maintenance history, vendor contacts and any notes on upcoming guests, so nothing gets lost.
5. Plan the handoff for a quiet week, and tell guests with upcoming stays who they'll hear from.

Switching is far easier when the listing is already in your name.
